Introduction 

Movement has energetic cost, leading bio systems to minimize/stereotypize behaviors
Every movement comes at an energetic cost. This fundamental constraint is intrinsic to the self-organizing dynamics through which movement patterns emerge in biological systems (Ferrer-i-Cancho et al., 2013; Rosen, 1967). Humans are no exception: we exhibit stereotyped patterns of behavior that emerge from metabolic, computational, cognitive, biomechanical or other constraints (Bernstein, 1967; Kelso, 1995; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). In other words, people often opt for variants of actions which minimize effort (in terms of, e.g., joints or muscles engaged, trajectory, or velocity) to reach a goal, hence optimal solutions. Such optimality has been proposed for gait (REF), arm-reaching movement (XX), decision-making (Kool et al., 2010), or social behavior – including language, or communication more broadly (Levshina, 2022; Zipf, 1949).

Minimal effort principles also occur in language-related systems
The universal pressure of language towards minimal effort has a long research tradition in linguistics and nascent fields (Gibson et al., 2019; Hawkins, 2014; Levshina, 2022; Levshina & Moran, 2021; Zipf, 1949). Communication is considered efficient if the signal producer “spends not more effort than necessary in order to convey intended information, while at the same time maximizing processing ease for the recipient(s)” (Levshina & Moran, 2021). Inspired mainly by information theory (Shannon, 1948), these accounts typically operationalize effort as the length of the transmitted message. Short messages resist noise more readily, require less articulation, and crucially, place fewer demands on processing (e.g., decoding, memory). Such conceived efficiency has been found to explain the structural tendency of frequent words in a lexicon to have short forms (Haspelmath, 2021; Zipf, 1949), word order (REF) and the position of syntactically related elements (Futrell et al., 2015), sound duration (Bell et al., 2009; Seyfarth, 2014), or the amount of articulatory and phonological detail (Aylett & Turk, 2004; Bell et al., 2003; Cohen Priva, 2008; Gahl & Garnsey, 2004). In speech sciences, physical effort has been used to explain the observations that vocal targets are regularly not fully realized, resulting in phonetic reductions (Kirchner, 2001; B. Lindblom, 1990).  

Comm effort research focused on abstract features, leaving multimodality untouched
These examples broadly reflect two facets of effort. Some work focuses on abstract linguistic features such as word choice and information structure, which we might consider cognitive effort: involving mental processes like lexical retriewval, planning and organizing information. Other work examines the mechanics of articulation in speech production, and less commonly, sign  (Napoli et al., 2014; Slonimska et al., 2022), which we might term physical effort: the biomechanical costs of muscular activation and bodily movement.  However, both lines of research have predominantly focused on single modalities, neglecting that language is a fundamentally multimodal phenomenon, involving hand gestures, eye gaze, facial expressions, head nods, postural sway, and more (Emmendorfer & Holler, 2025; Nota et al., 2021; Shockley et al., 2003; ter Bekke et al., 2024). Given that these signals emerge from bodily movement, they are also shaped by more general constraints of human physiology (REF TO MULT. CHAPTER?). Consider speech: it emerges from and is shaped by breathing (Fuchs & Rochet-Capellan, 2021) and biomechanics of tongue and mandible (REF). Head and arm gestures involve substantial mass that interacts with body systems crucial for speech production, such as the larynx (REF:Cwiek) and the lungs (Pouw et al., 2020). The forces generated by these movements are integral to their communicative function. Meanwhile, posture provides the stable foundation upon which all these bodily signals depend, shaping and constraining multimodal communication as a whole (REFs). Movement is therefore a primary component of communication and should play a significant role in what we call communicative effort (or efficiency). Yet the physical costs associated with whole-body movement in communicative interaction remain largely unexplored (as do the cognitive costs of coordinating these multiple modalities, though we do not address those here). This study addresses this gap by investigating how people employ whole-body physical effort during an interactive task to overcome a communicative challenge. We first establish the concept of effort, then review the evidence on articulatory effort in communicative behavior, and finally introduce our research question.

1.1. Deviating from minimal effort

We do not purely minimize effort, we adjust to environment
Despite the proposed universal pressure toward minimal effort, interlocutors do not always operate at this assumed minimum. In fact, people seem to systematically deviate from low-effort patterns in specific communicative contexts. For instance, speakers adjust loudness and clarity for addressees with hearing impairments or when in very noisy room. Effort minimization is therefore not absolute, but must be weighted against other imperatives. 
Lindblom’s model explains it as a trade-off
In his H&H Theory, Lindblom (1990) considered effort along a continuum between hyper- and hypo-articulation, arguing that speakers’ behavior results from a trade-off between general biological (optimizing) processes on the one hand and environmental constraints on the other. If the environmental constraints remain minimal (e.g., a listener has good conditions for sound discrimination), a motor system is inclined toward low-cost (hypo-) behavior, as opposed to extreme (hyper-) positions in articulation. In the current study, we aim to extend Lindblom’s framework by explicitly including other modalities that people employ in communication. Following his work, we view engaging in communication as a search for a balanced trade-off between the need of the speaker (i.e., control energy spent on producing a message) and the need of the listener (i.e., ensure that processing a message will not be too costly). If such a trade-off was not established, communication could not occur – or would break down. This is because the needs of the involved participants are, in some sense, contradictory. While the producer hypothetically aims for the least effort possible, i.e., to remain lazy (Kirchner, 2001) and produce minimal articulatory movement, the addressee could easily confuse such a movement or not recognize it at all. Seen from the other perspective, the ideal movement for a communicative partner in terms of intelligibility is, conversely, one that is articulated with maximal clarity (i.e., hyper-articulated). This trade-off represents a first layer of constraint on communication. Each participant’s needs, abilities, and states require adaptation: for instance, speakers  adjust loudness and clarity for addressees with hearing impairments. A second layer involves environmental constraints in the narrower sense:  auditory or visual noise requires adjustments to volume of speech, gesture, or sign (i.e., Lombard speech; REFs). Acknowledging these and other constraints might ultimately help to avoid a breakdown – or potentially reduce infinite repetitions which themselves add to the overall effort expended by the producer.

Interlocutors navigate trade-off in dynamic negotiation
How exactly interlocutors navigate this trade-off is not fully understood. However, we do not view this as primarily an internal computational process. Rather, we assume that speakers draw on information from the environment and their ongoing interaction, beginning with an initial calibration shaped by assumptions about the addressee – grounded in prior experience and social-cognitive abilities (e.g., Yang & Kita, 2024). Communication then unfolds as a dynamic, interactive process in which interlocutors continuously negotiate their respective needs to achieve mutual understanding (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986; Horton & Keysar, 1996), a genuinely joint activity requiring coordinated effort from all pupil involved (Bögels et al., 2024; Rasenberg et al., 2022). 

We can study this negotiation by looking at repair
One particularly revealing window into this negotiation process comes from repair sequences, i.e., moments when understanding has broken down and must be restored  (Schegloff et al., 1977). These repairs occur frequently, approximately every five minutes in natural conversation (Dingemanse et al., 2015). Rather than simply reflecting communicative failures, repairs constitute a universal cooperative system for managing the diverse challenges inherent to social interaction: from environmental noise to differences in knowledge, cultural background, attention, or perceptual abilities between interlocutors. Some may even originate from intentional ambiguity, a core functional property of language that allows for efficiency (Piantadosi et al., 2012). Ultimately, repairs are an integral and adaptive part of communication, and interlocutors frequently reconcile these breakdowns through adjustments in their behavior. Critically, these adjustments can take different forms: speakers may reorganize their message without necessarily increasing physical effort (e.g., rephrasing), or they may modulate their articulation to enhance clarity, which does carry physical costs (e.g., speaking more loudly or making bigger gestures). 1.2. Hyper-articulated adaptations to communicative needs

People hyper-articulate when interacting with ‘special’ interlocutors
An example of such a hyper-articulated behavior in interaction is communication with an infant or a child. Infant-directed speech is often exaggerated, slow-paced, and has a wider f0 range (Biersack et al., 2005; Song et al., 2010). Similarly, infant-directed bodily actions have been shown to consist of a larger range of motion, greater repetitiveness, and more segmented and less smooth movements in order to be more appropriate for a child's perceptual-cognitive abilities (Brand & Shallcross, 2008; Rohlfing et al., 2022). These patterns have also been observed in actions that serve for teaching. Żywiczyński and colleagues (2023) report exaggerated volume and slower speed of movement in demonstrations and pantomimes of an action used for instructions compared to instrumental actions. People also adapt their speech when interacting with a non-native addressee, for instance by lowering speech rate, increasing mean f0, f0pitch range, or intensity (Knoll et al., 2015; Smith, 2007).

People hyper-articulte when audio/visual-access is hindered
The tendency to compensate for situational constraints with hyper-articulation can also be seen when the environment hinders the auditory access to the information. For instance, when experiencing strong background noise, we generally speak louder (Garnier & Henrich, 2014), gesture larger, and enhance other visual signals (e.g., lip aperture, Dohen & Roustan, 2017; Trujillo et al., 2021). Furthermore, Beechey and colleagues (2020) observe more extreme formant frequencies in the vowel space corresponding to jaw opening and front vs. back articulation when participants interact with people with hearing impairment compared to typically hearing individuals. 

Speakers also adapt their effort depending on shared understanding
Beyond these clear-cut examples, it is quite common for people to adjust their bodies in social interactions more generally, reflecting the negotiation of a common ground, i.e., shared knowledge between interlocutors. For instance, Hilliard & Cook (2016) report that speakers add more details to their gestures when they are aware of a lack of shared understanding between them and their communication partner. Further, when people attempt to regain understanding, they tend to gesture with an increased number of submovements and speak longer (Rasenberg et al., 2022). Notably, while repetition typically leads to reduction (Ref: Bybe?), gestures do not reduce when interlocutors provide negative feedback (i.e., incorrect guess in a game), suggesting sustained effort to repair understanding (e.g., Hoetjes et al., 2015; see Holler & Bavelas, 2017 for a review). Indeed, gestures in repaired sequences become more precise (as opposed to sloppy) compared to their previous occurrence (Holler & Wilkin, 2011). On the other hand, several studies on co-speech hand gestures found that when common ground is established, one’s gestures can become shorter (Holler et al., 2022), smaller, less precise (Gerwing & Bavelas, 2004a), or less frequent (Holler & Stevens, 2007; Parrill, 2010). 

Effortful modulation is not reorganization
In this study, we are interested in how effort is employed when interlocutors face problems in achieving shared understanding in a referential task. The above-mentioned comparisons of somewhat default behavior and their modulated alternatives provide an initial ground for claiming that people depart from the hypothetical low-cost tendencies of their motor-muscular system to reach communicative goals and sustain meaningful interaction when common ground is not yet established or situations demand it. However, with the exception of the work of Beechey et al. (2020), these modulations have rarely been straightforwardly associated with physical effort per se. As mentioned, not all repairs must involve enhanced articulation – some employ mere reorganizations of the system (e.g., rephrasing an utterance in order to simplify or reapproach the meaning), which may not increase physical effort. In contrast, precise gestures require clear delineations of unique submovements such that the overall movement is less smooth, therefore less optimal (Wong et al., 2021). Similarly, loud speech requires more subglottal pressure, and hence more extreme activation of respiratory muscles (Seikel et al., 2015), and clear speech is achieved by expanding vowel space and longer duration (Ferguson & Kewley-Port, 2007; Hazan & Markham, 2004). Hence, these adaptations can be expected to come with an increased effort.

Effort is both cost and value
If this is the case, an intriguing tension emerges: how do principles of effort minimization coexist with the need to maintain meaningful social interaction? On one hand, biological imperatives push us toward efficiency. People minimize effort across many tasks, and may experience negative feelings such as frustration when engaging in more effortful or difficult tasks (David et al., 2024). Paradoxically, exerting effort is also crucial for mental and physical health, and lack of effort can lead to boredom or dissatisfaction (see Job et al., 2024 for a review). Furthermore, deliberately opting for challenging tasks, e.g., in play, sport, and other manual or cognitive tasks, is essential for learning or emotion regulation, and increases the value of its product – including social belonging (Inzlicht et al., 2018), and rewarding outcomes can also motivate people to put in more effort (Shadmehr et al., 2016; Summerside et al., 2018). This dual nature of effort – simultaneously a cost and a value – makes it particularly relevant for social interaction. In communication, increased effortfulness may signal positive acknowledgment of the interlocutor while also yielding personal gains. Consider storytelling with extensive body movements to entertain and bond with the others, versus an interaction where participants invest minimally in maintaining understanding, producing sparse movements. The question then becomes: when do interlocutors choose to invest effort in interaction, and how does this investment shape the interaction itself? 

1.3. Current study

The current study aims to investigate whether the modulations that people make in their communicative behavior to (re)gain understanding are associated with an increase in effort. Concretely, we ask:

How do people modulate their articulators regarding physical effort when they face a communicative challenge?

We view the increase in effortfulness as a shift from one’s low-cost baseline behavior to hyper-articulation, i.e., enhancement of articulatory activity. Importantly, this study focuses on physical effort and does not attempt to quantify cognitive effort. Yet, we acknowledge that cognitive and physical effort are deeply intertwined. Although cognitive effort is beyond the scope of the current analysis, it remains a crucial aspect of communicative efficiency.

While previous research shows that the body engages in altered behavior when experiencing communicative challenges (e.g., in repairs), we explicitly ask whether an increase in physical effort accompanies such modulations. We understand physical effort as an energetic cost of an action (Halperin & Vigotsky, 2024) associated with the enhancement of (a feature of) an articulator (B. Lindblom, 1990). We view both communication and the effort associated with it as whole-body phenomena. Therefore, we focus on three dimensions of the body – voice, upper limbs, and posture. To avoid confusion with the literature that uses various terms for each, we call them sound-producing (orofacial) movements, arm movements, and posture-related movements, respectively. At the same time, however, these dimensions serve purely the researcher’s purpose, as the human is one synergistic entity rather than a system of independent parts (Latash et al., 2007; Pouw et al., in press). 

Physical effort is principally a neuromuscular notion and refers to the total extent of activation that nerve impulses provide to voluntary muscle fibers in order for the motor system to exert forces (Borden & Harris, 1984). These activations consume adenosine triphosphate, the primary energy molecule that powers cellular processes (Alberts et al., 2002). However, measuring the activity of all muscles in the vocal tract and their potential coactivation and interactions remains impossible. Similarly, it has been concluded that oxygen consumption is an unsuitable proxy for the energetic cost of speech, due to the small overall energy expenditure and, thus, inappropriate time resolution (Moon & Lindblom, 2003). For that reason, researchers interested in effort during speech articulation have commonly conceptualized effort as a biomechanical cost. In these conceptions, effort is operationalized as a function of a performance variable that the motor system aims to optimize relative to the success of the goal. Previously used variables for speech include lower jaw peak velocity (Nelson, 1983) and peak acceleration (Nelson et al., 1984), or other kinematic proxies (e.g., formant peak velocity, Xu & Prom-on, 2019). Mere theoretically proposed variables further involve work (i.e., force × displacement, Björn Lindblom, 1990), or force (Kirchner, 2001).

To assess whether individuals truly recruit more physical resources when dealing with a misunderstanding rather than simply reorganizing their utterance, we extend previously used acoustic and kinematic measurements with biomechanical operationalizations of movement typically used in movement science such as torque (for limb movement) and center of pressure (for postural adjustment). They are not simple additions to a battery of features potentially associated with effort, but rather provide a promising link to expenditure of energy (Mohammadzadeh Gonabadi et al., 2024). 

We investigate the modulation of effort in a referential task in which dyads of people (performer and guesser) play a charade-like game wherein they pretend the absence of a common language. This paradigm has been widely used to explore the process of grounding the meaning of novel expressions built on various modalities such as drawing (e.g., Hawkins et al., 2023), gestures and vocalizations (Ćwiek et al., 2021; Fay et al., 2014, 2022; Macuch Silva et al., 2020), or other artificial systems (Selten & Warglien, 2007). We opt for a referential task via three modality conditions: performing participants express a concept to the guesser using only sounds, only body movements, or a combination of both (multimodal). This design allows us to assess how effort is affected by the degrees of freedom (one modality versus two) and how it is distributed across modalities. Importantly, we adapt the referential game to include feedback. If the reference for a concept is negotiated unsuccessfully (the guesser answers something else than the target meaning), the performer is informed about the incorrect answer of their partner. At this point, the dyad faces an interactive breakdown, and the performer has to express the same concept again. For our purposes, the first performance of a concept establishes a baseline, and the potential repairs that follow are what we call first and second corrections. 

The decision to design an experiment in which repair sequences are generated within the referential task and to use it as a testbed for modulation of physical effort has several motivations. In natural conversation, repair sequences often consist of a mix of linguistic and bodily cues (Rasenberg et al., 2022). While these changes can be identified, systematic comparison is complicated by the fact that successive expressions rarely refer to the same concept in the same way: people might change a word, simplify a phrase, add a gesture, or a facial expression, or a mix of these. This variability makes it difficult to isolate which specific modulations contribute to resolving the breakdown, and to compare expressions in terms of their relative effortfulness. In our study, bodily signals (including sound-producing, arm, and posture-related movement) carry the full weight of meaning-making. Moreover, the clearly delimited performances – beginning with the original proposal of an expression (i.e., baseline) and possibly continuing as repairs of this proposal (i.e., corrections) – allow us to systematically compare two successive expressions that refer to the same concept using comparable modalities, making differences in effortfulness directly measurable. 

We acknowledge that this controlled design comes with trade-offs in ecological validity. However, we view it as a necessary first step: by identifying and validating specific features of effort modulation in this simplified context, we establish a foundation for investigating these phenomena in more naturalistic settings. Furthermore, as Christianses and Chater (2022) argue, charade-like games share fundamental properties with linguistic interaction – they are inherently communicative, meaningful, and intersubjective.

1.4. Analyses overview

To answer our research question, we start by asking:

RQ1: Do misunderstandings recruit more physical effort?

In the task, a performer starts with a first performance of a concept (i.e., baseline). If the answer is guessed correctly, the performer can continue to the next concept. However, suppose the guesser answers anything other than the target concept. In that case, an interactive breakdown needs to be overcome – while the ultimate responsibility falls onto the performer and their following performance (i.e., correction). This challenge poses an output-oriented constraint (B. Lindblom, 1990) that asks for resolution in the form of hyper-behavior, i.e., behavior that is hyper-articulated compared to a baseline.

Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: Correction recruits more physical effort than the baseline performance.

In the following analysis, we adopt a two-fold approach. First, we investigate the increase in effortfulness by choosing three effort-related features based on the literature. In the more exploratory part, we consider other potentially relevant features as evaluated by a machine learning algorithm. In both, we use the identical causal and statistical model, the only difference is how we choose the outcome variable. While using features from the literature resembles more of a top-down approach, the exploratory analysis is essentially bottom-up.

Top-down analysis
To test H1, we will model the effect of a communicative attempt (i.e., baseline, first correction, second correction) on a feature of effort. We have one feature for each of the three investigated bodily dimensions – voice, upper limbs, and posture. For each dimension, we pick a feature previously used in the literature as relating to effort or for which we have a good reason to believe this feature is a good proxy. We choose intensity, torque change, and change in center of pressure, respectively. 

In the case of the human vocal tract, intensity is modulated by subglottal pressure and the degree of mouth opening (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009). Note that intensity does not directly relate to the biomechanics of the vocal tract and, therefore, is only an approximation of potential cost. However, a higher amplitude envelope peak does require generating more subglottal pressure (Zhang, 2016) and deeper engagement of breathing, including supporting muscles (Fuchs & Rochet-Capellan, 2021; Pouw et al., 2023), assuming a constant degree of jaw opening. We opt for intensity because it is the most stable measurement of voice throughout our task. Non-linguistic vocalizations do not always contain voicing, and therefore, using f0 (which would be a better proxy for subglottal pressure) or formants (as a proxy of articulatory space) would not yield consistent or reliable data. We are aware that the mapping between acoustics and speech articulation is not straightforward, but further employment of other articulatory methods would limit the naturalness of the task and, most importantly, freedom of movement.

Torque change has been previously used to model the optimal trajectory of an upper-limb movement (Uno et al., 1989). It is the first derivative of the force that a limb needs to generate to rotate around its joint. The minimal torque change model is somewhat similar to the minimal jerk model (Hogan, 1984), claiming that the goal of the motor system is to make the smoothest movement possible, i.e., movement which minimizes the rate of change of acceleration. While there has been criticism of such stated propositions, the torque change model, due to its account of nonlinear arm dynamics, does result in good performance (see (see Todorov & Jordan, 2002 for a review)). We opt for using torque change because, unlike kinematics (especially if extracted from computer vision video-based motion tracking), it allows us to account for the fact that body segments have different masses, and that moving the whole arm requires a different effort than moving only the hand.

Lastly, the center of pressure (COP) characterizes the concentrated point of ground reaction forces acting on a person’s feet. Thus, its trajectory informs of one’s postural control (Winter, 1995). Its first derivative – change in COP (COPc) – captures fluctuations and, therefore, reflects demands on sustaining a balance (i.e., not falling). It has been previously shown that articulation and increased vocal effort are accompanied by stronger postural sway (i.e., the movement of the body’s centre of mass while standing, e.g., trunk tilt, Lagier et al., 2010). While the COPc does not directly inform about the sway and has not been previously used as a measure of effort, the feet activity does relate to the sway corrections, and hence reflects whole-body effortfulness. Postural adjustments need to counterbalance perturbations caused by upper limb movement (e.g., gesturing), and any such movements therefore potentially increase further demands on the supporting trunk muscles, such as muscles in the back (erector spinae; Cavallari et al., 2016; Pouw et al., 2023; Zedka & Prochazka, 1997).

To fully capture the effort dynamics, we consider both cumulative and instantaneous effort for all these three measures. Cumulative effort reflects the total exertion over time, which is particularly relevant for sustained actions and overall physical demand. We quantify this by integrating each feature across time, providing a measure of accumulated intensity, torque change, and postural shifts. Instantaneous effort, on the other hand, highlights peak exertion at specific moments. By analyzing peak values of the signals, we can identify the local physical demands on the body. This dual approach allows us to differentiate between continuous exertion and sudden bursts of effort, offering a more comprehensive perspective on physical cost across communicative attempts. 

In addition to H1, we hypothesize that participants will efficiently use the information about the current state of the common ground to assess what the following correction will look like. As a consequence, the effortfulness in the correction might depend on the discrepancy between the guesser’s attempt and the target meaning. In other words, we anticipate that the magnitude of the effort (in a particular feature) will depend on the degree of misunderstanding. Concretely:

H2: A higher degree of misunderstanding will require a performer to engage in a more effortful correction.

This directly follows the assumption that common ground is crucial in effort minimization as it allows to omit information that is accessible (Levshina, 2022), supported by the evidence that interlocutors indeed exploit the situation to adapt their multimodal behavior, including the shared knowledge (Gerwing & Bavelas, 2004b; Hilliard & Cook, 2016; Holler & Wilkin, 2011). We assume that a better established common ground – using the guesser’s answer – will require less effort from the performer to level the gradient of misunderstanding (Thibault, 2022). We operationalize a degree of misunderstanding as a semantic distance between the guesser’s answer and the performer’s target concept. Using ConceptNet word embeddings (Speer et al., 2018), we calculate the cosine similarity between each answer-target pair, wherein values closer to 1 represent a better guess (i.e., smaller misunderstanding) and vice versa.

Bottom-up analysis
Because our data allow us to access more acoustic and kinematic information about the articulators engaged in the task beyond the three variables sketched above, we leave the door open for any other measurements associated with effort. We collect other effort-related, cumulative, and instantaneous features and model them using eXtreme Gradient Boosting algorithm (XGBoost; Chen & Guestrin, 2016). XGBoost is a machine learning algorithm that builds an ensemble of decision trees to predict outcomes (here, communicative attempt) based on input features (here, effort-related features). We use it in this study to identify the most relevant effort-related features. We run a model for each modality separately to get insights into potentially informative measurements of effort associated with behavior consisting of only sound, only bodily movement, and a combination of both. In parallel, we run a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on three modalities to assess which of these features contribute to which components. We do this to ensure that we assess the effort on multiple, uncorrelated dimensions, as XGBoost could potentially result in a list of the most predictive variables that are all strongly correlated. We then select the three most contributing features per component per modality and model them, similarly to the three features sketched above. We choose to focus on the top three features per the first three principal components, as this gives us enough data for a manageable yet comprehensive analysis of the most predictive features of effort.
